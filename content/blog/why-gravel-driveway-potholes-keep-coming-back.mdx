A gravel driveway pothole usually looks simple: low spot, rough edge, loose stone, annoying bump. The tempting fix is to shovel gravel into the hole and move on. Sometimes that helps for a little while. If the same pothole returns after every storm, though, the hole is only the symptom.

Most recurring potholes come from shape and water. When water sits in the travel lane, traffic pushes the saturated material around. Gravel gets displaced, fines pump out, and the depression gets deeper. The next rain fills the same spot again, and the cycle repeats.

If you are fighting the same holes over and over, the driveway likely needs [gravel driveway repair](/gravel-driveway-repair), [pothole repair](/pothole-repair), or [gravel driveway grading](/gravel-driveway-grading) before more stone will do much good.

## Water Is Usually The First Clue

Walk the driveway after a rain and look for standing water. A gravel driveway should shed water off the driving surface. If water sits in tire paths, potholes, entrance dips, or flat sections, the surface is telling you where the next repair will fail.

Common water problems include:

- The driveway is too flat and has no crown
- Tire paths are lower than the center of the drive
- Gravel has migrated to the shoulders
- Runoff travels down the driveway instead of off the side
- A culvert or ditch line is backing water toward the entrance
- Low spots hold water after every storm

Adding gravel into a wet low spot can hide the problem, but it does not always change the way water moves. That is why the repair may disappear into the same hole.

## Crown Matters More Than Most People Think

The crown is the slight high point near the center of a gravel driveway. It does not need to look dramatic, but it does need to move water toward the sides. Without crown, water follows traffic. Traffic then follows the water damage.

A driveway with poor crown often develops two low tire lanes with a ridge in the middle or loose gravel on the edges. Those low tire lanes become soft after rain. Once the base is soft, potholes form faster and repairs hold for less time.

This is where [driveway grading](/gravel-driveway-grading) helps. Grading can cut high spots, pull displaced stone back into the travel surface, restore crown, and blend rough areas so water leaves the drive more cleanly.

## Loose Gravel Migration Creates Thin Spots

A driveway can have plenty of gravel on it and still be thin where it matters. Stone often gets pushed into the shoulders, the center ridge, or the entrance. The travel lane then loses support. Once the usable gravel is thin, potholes and ruts show up faster.

Before ordering a fresh load, look at where the existing material is sitting. If usable stone has moved away from the driving path, [gravel redistribution](/gravel-redistribution) may reclaim some of that material. If the whole drive is thin, new gravel may still be needed, but it will work better after the surface is shaped.

## Soft Base Areas Need More Than A Top-Off

Some potholes return because the surface shape is wrong. Others return because the base underneath is weak. Soft spots are common near entrances, low areas, culverts, shaded wet sections, or places where water crosses the driveway.

Signs of a soft base include:

- A pothole that gets muddy or pumps fines after rain
- Gravel that disappears into the same spot repeatedly
- A low area that feels spongy under vehicle tires
- Edge washouts near a culvert or ditch
- Ruts that form quickly even after fresh gravel

A soft base may need more material, better drainage, or a repair that opens and rebuilds the weak area instead of just covering it.

## When More Gravel Helps

Fresh gravel can absolutely help, but timing matters. It works best when the driveway is shaped first and the problem areas are identified. If you add gravel before fixing crown, drainage, or migration, the new material can simply follow the same failure pattern.

Fresh gravel usually makes sense when:

- Existing gravel depth is genuinely too thin
- The surface has already been graded or prepared
- Low areas need enough material to blend properly
- The driveway needs a new top layer after repair
- Delivery and spreading are planned together

## What To Send For A Better Estimate

Clear photos make a driveway estimate much more useful. Take wide photos from both directions, then close-ups of the worst holes. If water is the issue, take photos after rain before the puddles dry.

Helpful photos include:

- The full driveway from the road
- Each recurring pothole or rut
- The driveway entrance
- Any culverts, ditches, or drainage paths
- Areas where gravel has moved to the side
- A close-up showing existing gravel depth
